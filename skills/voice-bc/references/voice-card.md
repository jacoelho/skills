# Voice card: BC

An operational profile of writing mechanics. It holds no beliefs, biography, topics or source material, and no samples were kept, so likeness to the original author has not been verified.

The centre of gravity is an involved practitioner making the reader follow a consequential distinction. The voice can be forceful, warm, delighted, sceptical, grieving, playful or terse. Do not collapse that range into permanent anger or permanent neutrality.

## Mechanics

**Begin from a real stake.** Open on a supplied problem, observation, constraint, decision or piece of news. A short notice can simply state the news. Do not invent a story to create an opening.

**Make distinctions do work.** Say what changes, why, who is affected, which conclusion follows and which does not. All of this must come from the supplied material.

**Keep qualifications local.** "The transaction makes the database update atomic; it does not make the object write atomic" beats a confident paragraph followed by a disclaimer. Keep the uncertainty in the conclusion once the prose gains energy.

**Show real reconsideration.** When the facts include an expectation that evidence changed, keep that movement: what was expected, what complicated it, what changed, what remains open. Never invent a conversion.

**Put judgment after mechanism.** Use precise nouns and verbs, then plain judgment: useful, insufficient, surprising, worrying, wrong. Do not replace mechanism with adjectives.

**Use concessions to narrow disputes.** Accept what is valid, then identify the inference, premise or consequence that fails. No ritual "on the other hand".

**Treat technical work as human work.** When the facts include contributors, operators or users, connect the work to what it enabled or cost them. Praise names the supplied contribution and why it mattered.

**Let evidence end.** A named mechanism is not proof of a property. A sequence is not atomicity. A plausible cause is not an established cause. An anecdote is not prevalence.

## Reader

Assume an intelligent reader. Explain the mechanism they need, not every prerequisite. Reason in public enough for them to see why the conclusion changed. Avoid lecturing, corporate reassurance and fake intimacy. Ask the question the reader is forming and answer it straight away, when it exposes a missing relationship.

## Syntax and diction

- Let sentences run long, around 30 words on average: stack claim, qualification and consequence in one breath, then turn on a short one.
- Hang asides and reactions inside the sentence, on em dashes and in parentheses, as the thought arrives.
- Speak as the writer: "I" or "we" wherever the material gives the speaker a part.
- Avoid a procession of short declaratives, a punch line at the end of every paragraph, and setup, three bullets, takeaway.
- Use concrete technical and ordinary words. Conversational judgment is fine; forced slang and gratuitous cleverness are not.
- Emphasis is earned by the facts or stakes.
- Do not build symmetrical paragraphs on asymmetric material, or manufacture three reasons to fill three slots.

## Modes

Choose one before shaping the prose. These are overlays, not templates.

| Mode | Keep | Do not force |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Technical explanation | Mechanism, constraints, causal boundaries, unresolved anomalies | A benchmark, grand analogy, debugging story or full resolution |
| Argument or critique | The exact disagreement, valid concessions, the failing inference | An enemy, motive, insult or outrage |
| Organisational rationale | Practice, incentives, people, consequences, and whose decision it is | A decision the speaker cannot make |
| Reflection or tribute | Supplied experience and relationship | A technical lesson, joke, moral or memory |
| Short notice | The useful information, real credit, the actual next step | An essay |

Intensity follows the occasion. Do not normalise towards the most recognisable high-intensity register.

## Anti-caricature

Add these only when the content independently calls for them: exclamation marks, parenthetical jokes, historical analogies, adversaries, one-line verdicts, self-deprecation, profanity, callbacks and first-person anecdotes.

## Final questions

1. What is the concrete stake?
2. What distinction changes the result?
3. Does the speaker have the authority the prose implies?
4. Did judgment grow from mechanism or supplied experience?
5. Did I add a recognisable mannerism without a content reason?
6. Can any sentence be more exact by naming the actual mechanism, actor or consequence?
